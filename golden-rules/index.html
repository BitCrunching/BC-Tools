Golden Rules

Practical, in-depth guides for getting the most out of every tool — the how, the why, and the mistakes worth avoiding.

1. The Complete Guide to Image Formats: JPG vs PNG vs WEBP

Each format solves a different problem. Picking the right one changes file size and quality more than almost any other choice.

FormatBest forGoodLimit
JPGPhotosSmall files. Opens everywhere.No transparency. Loses a little quality on every re-save.
PNGLogos, screenshots, textExact pixels. Full transparency.Large files for photos.
WEBPWebsitesLossy or lossless. Transparency. Usually smaller.Some older software cannot open it.
HEICiPhone photosHigh quality at a small size.Many non-Apple apps cannot open it. Convert reads it but does not write it.

What the sizes look like. The same photo was 3.8 MB as JPG and 15.4 MB as PNG, four times larger, for a difference most people cannot see. The flat graphic came out close to the same size in all three formats.

Quick rule. Photo with no transparency: JPG or WEBP. Transparency, text or sharp edges: PNG or WEBP. Smallest file on a modern site: WEBP.

2. How to Turn Photos into a PDF (and Why You'd Want To)

One PDF is easier to send than twelve photos. It opens right away in any browser or PDF viewer, and the pages stay in your order. A ZIP has to be downloaded and unpacked first. Many forms for jobs, claims and school work ask for a single PDF.

Steps. Drop in your images, click PDF, then click Convert and download. Each image becomes one page.

Order. Convert follows the order you added the files. Add them in the order you want the pages.

Quality. Images go in as they are. A low-resolution image stays low-resolution, and huge camera photos make a heavy PDF. Compress or resize them first if you will email it.

Wrong tool? To merge PDFs you already have, use Combine instead.

3. Extracting Images from a PDF: A Practical Guide

Pull a page out of a PDF as an image. People do it to send one slide, edit a scanned page, reuse a chart, or archive pages as separate files.

How. Drop a PDF into Convert. The options change to JPG, PNG and WEBP. Each page becomes its own image, numbered by page. More than 5 pages download as one ZIP.

FormatPick it when
JPGYou want small files.
PNGThe pages have sharp text.
WEBPYou want a balance of both.

Resolution. Pages are rendered at a fixed size that suits screens. That is enough for digital use. For very fine print work, check the result first or use a tool made for high-resolution extraction.

4. When to Convert Your Images: Real-World Scenarios

Match the format to what you need to do.

SituationBest formatWhy
A platform names a required formatWhatever it requiresCheck its rules first to avoid a rejected upload.
Speeding up your own websiteWEBPSmaller files load faster.
Needing a transparent backgroundPNG or WEBPJPG cannot store transparency.
Print or professional usePNGIt avoids the blur JPG leaves around text and lines.
Archiving for the long termPNG, or lossless WEBPJPG loses a little quality on every re-save.

Conversion changes the file type, not the picture. A JPG's solid background stays solid in PNG. Removing a background needs a background-removal tool.

5. Common Conversion Mistakes (and How to Avoid Them)

Re-saving JPG as JPG. Each save loses a little more quality. Edit in PNG and export to JPG once, at the end.

Using JPG when you need transparency. "Why does my logo have a white box?" JPG cannot store transparency, and no setting changes that. Both the source and the output must be PNG or WEBP.

Converting up. A 400 × 300 JPG saved as PNG is still 400 × 300. Conversion adds no detail.

Choosing PNG for photos. In our test the PNG was four times larger than the JPG. Save PNG for transparency, text and sharp edges.

Not checking the output. Open the file before you send it somewhere important. Check page order, transparency and file size.

1. How Image Compression Actually Works

Compression makes a file smaller in one of two ways. Compress uses the lossy way for photos.

TypeFormatsWhat happens
LossyJPG, WEBPRemoves detail your eyes barely notice. The file shrinks to a fraction of its size. The removed detail is gone for good, and compressing again removes more.
LosslessPNGStores the same pixels more efficiently, like a ZIP file. Nothing is lost, but photos stay large.

Why PNG photos become JPG. A photo saved as PNG barely shrinks. As a JPG it can be a fraction of the size, with no visible difference. Screenshots, logos and sharp text are different: lossy compression blurs their edges. Keep those as PNG.

The quality slider. Compress has three levels: Low, Medium and High. Low removes the least detail and High removes the most. The first steps down from top quality save the most, and later steps save little. Most of the saving comes early, so the highest level is rarely worth it.

How to judge the result. Open the compressed file at the size you will actually use it. Lost detail shows first in smooth areas such as sky and skin, as blockiness and color banding.

2. Choosing the Right Compression Level for Your Use Case

Choose the level by where the image is going. On one 3.8 MB photo, Low saves about a fifth, Medium about two thirds and High about three quarters. Results vary by photo.

DestinationLevelWhy
Social media / messaging appsMediumThe app compresses it again anyway.
Your own website or online storeMedium to HighSmaller files load faster.
Print or professional client deliveryLow, or skip compressionPrint shows flaws a screen hides.
Long-term archivingLowLost detail never comes back.
Email attachment size limitsHighMany providers cap attachments around 25 MB.

Rule of thumb. Social apps and messengers compress again, so Medium is enough. For print and archives, keep a full-quality master, because removed detail cannot come back.

If you are unsure, start with Medium. Check the result, then go one level up or down.

3. Compression for the Web: Speed Without Sacrificing Quality

Resize first, then compress. The same photo at Medium is 1.2 MB at full size and 97 KB at 800 pixels wide.

Match the width to the display size. A picture shown 400 pixels wide does not need 3000. Resize it to about the size it appears, then compress. Sharp screens show about twice as many pixels as the space they fill, so a 400-pixel slot looks best with an 800-pixel image.

Pick the format. Photos: JPG or WEBP at Medium or High. WEBP is often smaller than JPG at the same look, and every modern browser opens it. Graphics, screenshots and text: PNG.

Stop at the sweet spot. Past Medium, each step saves little and the damage grows. Check the result at its real display size.

Use one level per batch. The same level for the same type of image keeps the whole site consistent.

4. When to Compress vs When to Leave an Image Alone

Compress when the file is too big for where it is going. Skip it when quality matters more.

SituationWhat to doWhy
Camera photo for web, email or chatCompressIt carries far more detail than those places show.
File over an upload or email size limitCompressIt gets under the limit without cropping.
Screenshot, logo, diagram or scan with textLeave as PNG, or compress very lightlyLossy compression blurs edges and text.
A JPG that is already compressedDo not compress it againIt barely shrinks and gains more artifacts.
The only copy of a photoKeep a master copy firstA lost original cannot be recreated.

Rule of thumb. Keep one high-quality original and compress copies of it, one for each use. Give each copy its own name, such as photo-small.jpg, so you never overwrite the original.

5. Common Compression Mistakes (and How to Avoid Them)

Compressing the same file again and again. Each pass can remove more detail. In our test the size stayed at 1.2 MB through ten passes, so repeating gains nothing. Always start from the original.

Overwriting your only copy. Compress makes a new file and leaves your original alone. The risk is saving the new file over the old one, or deleting the original afterwards. Keep it until you are sure you will not need it at higher quality.

Treating graphics like photos. Text and logos smudge under photo compression. Use a light level, or keep PNG.

Not checking the result. Maximum compression often adds visible artifacts. Check the output at the size it will be viewed.

Over-preparing for apps that compress again. Instagram and WhatsApp recompress every image, so a huge file only slows your upload.

Combine merges your files in the order of its list, top to bottom. It never guesses from file names, dates or content.

Drag to reorder. Drag any file up or down (by touch on a phone) and the merged PDF follows. PDFs go in as they are, and every JPG or PNG becomes one page.

Check the previews. Each file shows a preview of its page. Look at the list before you download. It takes thirty seconds and saves a redo.

File names can fool you. A file picker may sort names the way a computer does, not the way a person does.

One PDF is easier to review than four attachments. One click opens everything in the order you meant.

Scans often have names like scan001.pdf that do not match the page order.

Check the page, not the name. Look at each preview before you merge. It is the surest way to avoid sending a scrambled document to a bank or an office.

Mixed scans and photos work. PDFs and JPG or PNG images go in together. A phone photo can look different from a real scan, so re-scan the odd one out if looks matter.

Sideways pages. Use the rotate button on that file. It turns a quarter at a time and the preview updates at once.

Signature pages. A signature page in the wrong place can make a contract look incomplete. Check it first.

Combine files that belong together. Keep the rest apart.

A big batch of unrelated reference filesKeep separate, or ZIPThe reader can open just the one they need.

Quick test. Is it one thing to read from start to finish? Combine it. Is it several things that just travel together? Keep them apart.

5. Common Combining Mistakes (and How to Avoid Them)

Trusting file names for the order. Names do not always match the order you want. Look at the list before you download.

Mixing unrelated documents. One big file makes the reader scroll to find their part. Combine only what belongs together.

Not checking the final file. Skim the merged PDF once. It catches an out-of-order page or a duplicate.

Leaving a page sideways. Rotate it in the list. The preview shows the result at once.

Sending a huge file. Merged files inherit the weight of every page. Set the image quality to Smaller or Smallest and check the size on each file.

1. What EXIF Metadata Actually Reveals About You

Every photo carries a hidden block of data called EXIF. It records when, where and how the photo was taken.

Hidden dataWhat it showsPrivacy weight
GPS locationThe place the photo was taken, often within a few metersHighest
TimestampThe exact date and time, down to the secondHigh
Device make and modelThe camera or phone usedSome
Camera settingsAperture, shutter speed, ISO, focal lengthLow

It adds up. One photo seems harmless. Several photos together can map the places you visit often: home, work, the gym, a child's school.

What Cleanly does. It strips this data before a photo leaves your device. The picture looks exactly the same.

2. How to Strip Metadata Before Selling or Posting Photos

Photos that go to strangers are the ones where hidden data matters most.

Selling online. Listing photos are usually taken at home. Left alone, they can hand a buyer your address before you have even met.

Posting online. Many platforms strip metadata on upload, but not all. Downloaded or reshared copies can keep whatever survived.

Make it a habit. Run photos through Cleanly before you upload them anywhere public. It takes seconds and works on a whole batch.

iPhone photos (HEIC). Cleanly cannot read inside HEIC, so it always flags them. When you clean one, it becomes a PNG or JPG, as you choose on its row.

What it does not change. Anything you can see in the picture, like a street sign or a house number. Check that with your own eyes.

1. How Adding Text to a PDF Actually Works

A PDF page is like a printed sheet: a fixed picture of text and shapes at exact spots.

No fields to fill. Most PDFs have no empty space you can type into. A line or a box is just ink on the page.

Context adds a layer. It places new text on top of the page, wherever you drag it. It does not find a hidden field.

It is not editing. Changing text that is already printed needs special software, and is often impossible. An overlay is what you want for notes, captions, initials and blank lines.

Points, not pixels. PDF positions use points (1/72 of an inch). A box stays where you put it at any zoom or on any device.

When you download. Every text box, date, shape and signature is drawn into the pages for good. You get an ordinary PDF that opens the same anywhere.

2. Choosing the Right Font Size and Placement for Different Documents

The right size depends on the document and who reads it next.

DocumentWhat to do
Filling in a form's blank linesMatch the printed label's size. Sit just above the line.
Adding a note or commentUse a contrasting color. Keep it clear of the original text.
Signing or initialingSlightly larger than nearby text, centered on the line.
Captioning an image-heavy PDFSame size for every caption on every page.
Formal or legal documentsMatch the document's own size, in black.

Match the printed text. Forms are often printed at about 11 to 12 pt. An answer at 24 pt looks pasted on. Context remembers your last size, so match it once.

Stay inside the lines. Shrink a long answer to fit, so it does not run past the printed line.

Pick a readable color. Black is safest. Use color for notes, with strong contrast. Pale yellow disappears on white.

Leave some margin. Keep text off the page edge, in case it is printed on different paper.

3. Filling Out Forms and Signing Documents Without a Printer

Print, sign, scan and email back is rarely needed. A browser does the job.

Skip the paper loop. Put a text box on each blank, type, and download. For a checkbox, use a checkmark, crossmark or circle shape instead of typing a symbol.

Dates in one click. Add date puts today's date in as text, in the last format you used. Double-click it to flip between day-first and month-first.

Signatures. Upload a photo of your signature, take one with the camera, or draw it. Crop it, brush away leftovers, fade faint marks and pick an ink color. Then place and resize it.

Be careful. A signature image is not a legally recognized electronic signature under laws like eIDAS or the U.S. ESIGN Act. For contracts and legal filings, use an e-signature service.

Line it up. Put your text just above the printed line, not on top of it.

Check at real size. Zoom through every page before you send it.

4. Working with Multi-Page PDFs: Consistency and Page-by-Page Editing

Long documents need consistency more than speed.

Boxes stay on their page. A box added on page 3 stays on page 3, however many pages you visit.

Stay consistent. Your last size and color carry to the next box. For a label that repeats, copy the box with Ctrl/Cmd+C and paste it with Ctrl/Cmd+V on the next page. Ctrl/Cmd+Z undoes it.

Look first. Skim every page before you type. A size that fits a sparse page can be too big for a dense one.

Review before you download. Page back through from the start. It catches a skipped page or a box that looks different.

5. Common Mistakes When Adding Text to PDFs (and How to Avoid Them)

Text too small. It can look fine zoomed in and tiny at full size. Zoom back out before you download.

Text on top of printed text. Both become hard to read. Use empty space nearby.

Editing the wrong page. Check the page indicator before you add a box, especially after navigating.

A color with poor contrast. Pale colors fade when printed. Use black or a strong color for anything that must be read.

Forgetting to rename. Press R to rename the file before you download. A clear name saves confusion later.

A file too big to email. Over 10 MB, Context shows a Compress file button. It shrinks JPEG images only, so a file that is big for other reasons may not shrink much.

1. The Complete Guide to GIFs: Frame Rate, Size, and Why They're Still Everywhere

A GIF is a silent loop of still pictures. That is why it plays by itself almost everywhere.

What a GIF is. A series of images, each with at most 256 colors, played in a loop. There is no sound, ever. A sunset with soft gradients can show banding.

Frame rate. Congify offers 5, 10, 15 and 20 frames per second. Each frame is one more image in the file.

Frames per secondUse it for
5Long or mostly still clips, like a text slide or a slow pan
10Most clips. A good default.
15 to 20Fast motion, like gameplay or quick gestures

Crop. Full keeps the original shape. Square crops to a centered square, which most feeds show cleanly. Portrait suits stories. Whatever is cropped away is gone for good, so check the preview.

2. Trimming: Picking the Right Clip Length

Trim tight. How much footage you use changes the file more than any setting.

Shorter is better. A GIF loops, so a good one earns a second look. A 6-second trim is roughly twice the size of a 3-second trim at the same settings.

Use the handles. Drag the two handles on the filmstrip to set the start and end. The preview updates live. Scrub slowly near both ends to cut dead air.

DirectionWhat it does
NormalPlays once through, then jumps back to the start. It can look abrupt.
ReversePlays backward, mostly as an effect.
BoomerangPlays forward, then backward, so there is no jump at the loop point.

Speed is not frame rate. Speed sets how fast the GIF plays, such as 0.5x for slow motion or 2x and 4x for faster. Frame rate sets how many frames exist. A slow GIF at a low frame rate looks choppy, so pair slow with a higher rate.

3. Adding Captions That Actually Work

GIFs have no sound, so captions carry the message. Moving footage makes them harder to read.

FontBest for
ImpactMemes. Clear at small sizes over busy backgrounds.
ArialA neutral, informational look.
Times, Comic SansWhen the tone calls for it. Weaker over moving footage.

Use a stroke. An outline in a contrasting color keeps text readable as the footage behind it changes. Turn it on for any real motion.

Pick strong contrast. White or yellow text with a black stroke works almost everywhere. Check the darkest and lightest moments of your clip.

Place it anywhere. Drag a caption to move it and drag its corner to resize it. You can add several, but one well-placed line is often enough.

4. When to Use a GIF vs an MP4 vs a Static Image

A video clip does not always need to become a GIF.

SituationBest choiceWhy

One moment, no motion neededA still imageIt is smaller and simpler.

Email and slides. Support for animated GIFs is uneven. Some apps show only the first frame. Test in the real place first.

5. Common GIF Mistakes (and How to Avoid Them)

Always using the highest frame rate. The smoothness gain over 10 is small for most clips, but the file gets much bigger.

Trimming too loosely. Extra seconds of lead-in make the file bigger and the point weaker. Trim tighter than feels comfortable.

Captions with no stroke. They can vanish once the background changes. Keep the stroke on.

Ignoring size on mobile. The whole GIF has to arrive before it plays. If it is big, trim it shorter and lower the frame rate first.

1. The Complete Guide to Audio Formats: MP3 vs WAV

Audio formats are lossy or lossless, like image formats. Pick by whether you want to share the sound or keep it to edit.

FormatTypeBest forCatch

2. Bitrate Explained: Choosing the Right Number for MP3

Bitrate is how much data encodes each second of sound, in kilobits per second (kbps). More bitrate keeps more detail and makes a bigger file. Doubling it roughly doubles the size.

BitrateOne minuteGood for

320 kbpsAbout 2.4 MBThe best an MP3 can do. Preselected in Coudio.

Both MP3 and OGG. Coudio offers 128, 192 and 320 for each.

Where it stops mattering. Past a point, most people cannot hear the difference on normal headphones or speakers. The file just gets bigger. 320 is about 70% bigger than 192.

WAV has no bitrate. It is not compressed, so there is nothing to choose.

3. Extracting Audio from a Video File

Coudio can pull the sound out of a video and leave the picture behind.

1. HEX, RGB, HSB, and HSL: What Each Color Code Actually Means

Every color on a screen is red, green and blue light. HEX, RGB, HSB and HSL are four ways to write the same color. Here is one orange in all four.

FormatSame orangeBest for
HEX#FF8A4CCSS and design tools. One short code.
RGBrgb(255, 138, 76)Code that works on separate red, green and blue numbers.
HSBhsb(21°, 70%, 100%)Changing brightness or saturation while keeping the hue.
HSLhsl(21°, 100%, 65%)Making a color lighter or darker for hover and active states.

HEX. Six digits that pack red, green and blue. Compact and accepted almost everywhere, but hard to read at a glance.

RGB. The same three channels as plain 0 to 255 numbers. Easier to adjust by hand.

HSB and HSL. Both start with hue, the color's place on the wheel. The other two numbers say how vivid and how light it is. For the same color, but darker, change one number and keep the hue.

In Colorfy. The format dropdown changes every readout at once. Pick the one you are about to paste.

2. Matching a Color From a Screenshot, Photo, or Design Reference

Guessing a color by eye rarely lands exactly. Sampling the real pixel does.

Close is not the same. Two colors that look alike can differ in one channel. You notice only when they sit side by side. The picker reads exactly what is in the file.

Use a screenshot. Drop in a screenshot of a page or app, drag the picker to the pixel you want, and copy the code. It works when you do not have the original file.

Beware photos and compression. A compressed JPEG, or a photo under warm light, shifts the real values. A logo photographed under yellow light samples warmer than it is. Use a clean screenshot when accuracy matters.

Sample a few spots. A solid-looking area can vary from pixel to pixel. Two or three pickers show if it is real variation or noise.

5. Common Color-Picking Mistakes (and How to Avoid Them)

Sampling on an edge. A pixel on a boundary is a blend of two colors. Zoom in and aim for the solid middle.

Trusting a compressed image. JPEG noise can shift a pixel. Use a PNG or other lossless source when exact color matters.

Forgetting your own screen. The code is exact, but screens show color differently. Check against a physical reference if it must match print.

Mixing projects in one palette. Saved colors stay across images. Shift + click the × button to clear them when you start something new.

1. How Video Compression Actually Works

A video is thousands of still frames, and most of each frame matches the one before. An encoder keeps what changes and stores the rest cheaply. Its main dial is bitrate: how many bits it spends on each second of video.

LevelBest for
LowArchiving, editing, watching on a big screen
MediumMessaging apps, email, uploading to a website
HighQuick previews, drafts, a strict upload limit

Why you see an estimate first. Each level sets a target bitrate from the video's size and frame rate. The estimate is that bitrate times the video's length. It is usually close. Busy footage can land a little above, and calm footage a little below.

2. Level or Size: What Shrinks a Video the Most

Both make the file smaller, in different ways. The level lowers the bitrate. Scaling down also cuts the number of pixels, so the same bitrate is spread over fewer of them.

When to scale down. A phone or chat window gains nothing from 4K. Dropping a 4K or 8K clip to a smaller size usually saves more than any level change. Keep the original size for big screens or more editing.

Portrait videos. Each smaller size is set by the shorter side. A vertical video scaled to 720 is 720 pixels wide, not tall. The shape always stays as filmed.

3. Why Phone Videos Are So Big

Phones favor speed and battery over file size. They record at a very high bitrate, often in 4K or at 60 frames per second. One minute can reach hundreds of megabytes.

Where the size comes from. Three things multiply: pixels per frame, frames per second, and bits spent on each. A phone screen or chat window cannot show most of it, so scaling down often saves the most.

MOV and MP4. These are containers, the box that holds the video and audio. They are not the compression. Comprio reads MOV, MP4, WebM and other common containers. It always writes a standard H.264 MP4, or WebM if your browser cannot encode H.264.

4. Keeping Quality When You Compress

Compressing is a one-way trade. Detail you remove cannot be recovered.

Keep the original. Comprio always saves a new file and never touches the original. Check the copy before you delete anything from your phone.

Do not compress twice. It loses quality again for little extra saving. If it is still too big, go back to the original and pick a higher level or a smaller size.

Check for tiny savings. Screen recordings and clips saved from social apps may barely shrink. Comprio shows the before and after size on each video.

The little "i". It appears when a video's bitrate is already low. The estimate assumes the encoder can go lower, but on busy footage it often cannot. The file can come out bigger than estimated and save very little. If the saving is small, keep the original.

5. Compressing in Your Browser: What to Expect

Comprio re-encodes the video on your own device with your browser's encoder. Nothing is uploaded and no server is involved.

Speed. It depends on your computer. A modern laptop with a hardware encoder often works faster than the video plays.

Keep the tab open. Browsers slow or pause background tabs. Leave the tab visible until the progress bar finishes. Pressing the button again cancels.

Very large files. The result is built in memory before saving. For a multi-gigabyte video, use one tab with nothing else running, or trim it first.

8K and odd sizes. Browsers cannot save 8K or odd-pixel sizes (like 1281×721) as MP4. Comprio saves a WebM at the exact same size and tells you. Choosing a smaller size on the preview gives you an MP4 again.

1. Containers and Codecs: What a Video Format Really Is

A video file has two layers. The codec is how the picture and sound are compressed (H.264, HEVC, VP9, AAC). The container is the box that holds them (MP4, MOV, MKV, WebM, AVI, FLV).

FormatBest for
MP4Sharing and uploading. Plays on almost every phone, computer and app.
MOVApple devices and editors like Final Cut, Premiere and DaVinci Resolve.
WebMWebsites. Small files that play in every modern browser.
MKVArchiving and players like VLC. Holds almost anything.
AVIOld players and software that only know AVI.
FLVOld Flash-era players and legacy streaming.

Which codec fits where. MP4, MOV, MKV, AVI and FLV take H.264 video with AAC audio, so a typical phone clip is simply re-packed. WebM takes VP8, VP9 or AV1 with Opus or Vorbis, so H.264 going into WebM must be re-encoded, and so must WebM going into MP4 or MOV.

Extensions can mislead. Renaming .mov to .mp4 converts nothing. Convido reads what is inside the file, not its name.

2. Re-pack or Re-encode: Fast and Lossless vs Slower

When the video and audio already fit the new format, Convido just re-packs them. Nothing about the picture or sound changes.

CaseWhat Convido doesResult
The contents already fit, like a phone MOV to MP4Re-packsInstant and lossless
H.264 or HEVC going into WebMRe-encodesSlower. The picture is compressed again.

How fast is fast? A 143 MB, ten-minute video can be re-packed from MOV to MP4 in well under a second.

The little "i". It appears when a video will be re-encoded. Pick a format that keeps it as it is, like MP4, MOV or MKV for most camera and phone videos, and the note disappears.

File size. A re-encoded file can come out smaller or larger. A tightly compressed video can grow. If it is too big, choose a format that re-packs, or shrink it afterwards with Comprio.

3. Choosing the Right Output Format

When in doubt, choose MP4. It works on phones, computers, browsers, messaging apps and social platforms.

WebM and MP4 are not interchangeable. WebM videos usually hold VP9 or AV1 with Opus audio. QuickTime, iPhones and many editors cannot play those inside MP4 or MOV. So Convido re-encodes to H.264 and AAC, and the result plays everywhere. MKV keeps the original codecs and re-packs instantly, which suits VLC.

AVI and FLV. Convido writes both with H.264 and AAC. AVI has no timestamps, so a changing frame rate can drift in some players, and an AVI file cannot be larger than 4 GB. Use them only when an old player asks.

No WMV. Browsers cannot encode WMV. For Windows, MP4 works in Windows Media Player and every current Windows app.

4. What Convido Can and Can't Read

Convido reads MP4, M4V, MOV, 3GP, 3GPP, 3G2, MKV, WebM, TS, M2TS, MTS and GIF. The Expected Input picker fills from the first video you add, and every file is recognized on its own, so you can mix formats.

Browsers cannot open these. AVI, FLV, WMV, ASF, MPG, MPEG, VOB, MOD, MXF, DV, DVR, RM, RMVB, SWF, CAVS and OGG/OGV. Convido skips them and says so. Camera RAW and audio-only files are skipped too.

Readable box, unreadable codec. Some files use a codec your browser cannot decode, such as certain old phone formats. They show a red card and the rest of the batch carries on. Old 3GP files with AMR audio may convert without sound.

GIF to video. Convido decodes every frame and encodes a normal video with the original timing. Transparent areas become white. MP4, MOV, MKV, AVI and FLV need an even size, so an odd-sized GIF gains a one-pixel white edge. WebM keeps the exact size. GIF input needs a browser with a built-in image decoder, like Chrome or Edge.
